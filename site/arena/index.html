Who fails first?

Three real-time games get harder until every player fails. On each seed, every player faces the identical schedule; the one who stays alive longer wins that head-to-head. The leaderboard is the share of head-to-heads a player won.

Read this first: a screen, not a verdict

Board 1 uses 10 seeds per game. That is enough to screen, not to settle close comparisons: no “X beats Y” claim is made from it. The Nagi entry at the top was picked as the best of four Nagi entries, which inflates its numbers (winner’s curse). That is why a separate, preregistered confirmation against Jev was run on fresh seeds; it passed, and it is reported in its own panel with its own n.

      03 / How to read the results

      One question per seed: who lasted longer?

      Difficulty is a fixed function of the game clock, never of what a player does. So on a given seed, every player faces exactly the same ramp, and comparing two players on that seed is fair even though the ramp itself is uneven.

      Primary · paired win rate

      For each pair of players and each seed: fail later = 1, same tick or both alive at 60 s = ½. Average over opponents, then over the three games.

      It does not depend on how steep the ramp is, so seconds in an easy level and seconds in a hard level are never mixed.

      Secondary · survival and level

      Survival curve: % of rounds still alive at each second (Kaplan–Meier). RMST: the area under that curve up to 60 s, in seconds. Level: the 10-second band (1–6) where the player failed.

      Rounds alive at 60 s are censored, not counted as failures. In Board 1, none reached 60 s.

      Anchors · the bot ladder

      Three fixed bots set the scale in every game: naive fails around Level 2, heuristic around Level 3, a search planner around Level 5. The bots play with the same one-tick reply delay the models get.

      Ladder position maps a survival time to 0 (naive), 0.5 (heuristic) and 1 (planner).

      Worked example. On Rotorwash-Ramp seed 0, T-dual Burst crashed at 24.8 s and Jev at 20.1 s: T-dual Burst scores 1 for that pair, Jev 0. Over 10 seeds, T-dual Burst outlasted Jev on 9, so its Rotorwash-Ramp rate against Jev is 90%. Its game score is the mean of such rates against all five opponents.

      Fail-first, real time, same clock

      06 / Speed is part of the score

      Latency is measured, never subtracted

      A reply is applied at the first 250 ms tick after it arrives, so a slower model acts on older information. A fresh action is one decided on the latest observation. Nagi and SemIf-4B run on the same pinned H100 path with three game streams per GPU; Jev is a remote API and its times include the network.

      Decision latency and freshness per player and game

      p50 = median decision latency, end to end (prompt, forward pass, readout; network for Jev). Fresh = share of live ticks controlled by a reply decided on the latest observation. Stale = replies older than 1 s, rejected. Resets = controls fell back to safe defaults after 1 s without an applied reply. 3-vs-1 = median-latency inflation with three concurrent streams versus one. T-dual Burst and T-dual K-call share weights: the difference is the readout, one forward pass for all controls versus one call per control.

      07 / Rules and fairness

      Same seeds. Same clock. Frozen rules.

      • Same schedule for everyone

        Difficulty depends only on the game clock. Seeds 0–9 are committed per game and shared by every player.

      • Real time at 4 Hz

        A decision every 250 ms, one request in flight per game stream. Replies apply at the next tick; the world never waits.

      • Stale and hold rules

        A reply older than 1,000 ms is rejected. Controls declare hold-or-one-shot behaviour; after 1,000 ms without an applied reply they reset to safe defaults. Before the first reply: defaults.

      • Void rounds

        If the harness processes any tick more than 100 ms late, the round is void: replayed once, then dropped for both sides of every pair. More than 1 void in 10 rounds invalidates the whole cell. Board 1 run 2 had 0 voids.

      • Pinned serving

        Self-hosted players on H100, merged bf16 weights, fused kernels, greedy readout, 3 streams per GPU with request micro-batching gated by a parity check against single-request answers.

      • Exact replays

        All 180 scored rounds (6 players × 3 games × 10 seeds) were replayed from the records and matched exactly. Clips are drawn only from verified records.

      • One attempt per seed

        No reruns conditioned on outcomes. Run 1 of Board 1 was stopped for a harness defect and is never pooled with run 2.

      • Training disclosure

        Nagi’s T-dual and ENORMOUS-CL entries were trained on similar control tasks (intended, disclosed below). Training on the arena engines, seeds or states is forbidden; the audit found none.

      In-domain training per player and game
      In-domain training disclosure

      “Near-transfer” training on similar machine-control tasks is informational and never changes a rate. External players’ training data is not auditable by us. Booster Gauntlet’s new skills (hoverslam without hover, multi-phase hops, relights) make it the least-seen game for every entry.

      08 / Read before quoting

      What this does and doesn’t show

      • A screen. n = 10 seeds per game (seed indices 0–9). The intervals are wide; the ranking of neighbours with overlapping intervals is not established.
      • Winner’s curse. T-dual Burst was selected as the best of four Nagi entries by a frozen rule, so its Board 1 numbers are optimistic. That is why the preregistered confirmation uses fresh seed indices 10–33 and only one comparison (Burst vs Jev). It passed on the composite; per game, only Booster Gauntlet is significant. It is reported separately and never pooled with seeds 0–9.
      • CLM-8B is not ranked because it failed its own conformance gate before play. The champion rule therefore used two external competitors (Jev, SemIf-4B), not three.
      • Survival is ordinal. Five seconds at Level 2 are not five seconds at Level 5, which is why the primary metric compares players seed by seed instead of averaging seconds.
      • Clustered end times. Many Snake Rush rounds end at exactly 10.0 s (the opening hunger limit) and many Booster Gauntlet rounds at exactly 22.0 s (the first hop’s landing deadline). All 25 Booster rounds that ended at exactly 22.0 s, including all 10 of T-dual Burst’s, were hop timeouts: the booster was still flying under control but had not landed its first hop. So its Booster lead comes from not crashing before that deadline, not from landing. Players who fail on the same tick tie; nothing is re-scored.
      • Latency is included. This measures a served product at 4 Hz on the stated hardware, not model quality in isolation. Jev’s hardware and network path are outside our control.
      • Our benchmark, our models. Nagi’s developers built the games and ran the evaluation. The game engines and raw records are not part of this release, to keep the benchmark sealed; the rules, per-seed outcomes and the receipt hash are.
