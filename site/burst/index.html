New · Nagi Burst · {{RELEASE_DATE}}

Every control.
One forward pass.

Burst is a readout for Nagi-ENORMOUS (27B) that sets all K controls of a real-time machine from a single forward pass, instead of one pass per control. The weights and the output (a probability for every option of every control) are the same. It helps when a machine has several controls and the world does not wait for the model.

Preregistered realtime confirmation, {{P2_N_SEEDS}} fresh seeds per game × 3 games: Burst survived longer than Jev in {{P2_WINRATE_VS_JEV}} of head-to-heads (95% CI {{P2_WINRATE_VS_JEV_CI}}) and longer than the same weights read one control at a time in {{P2_WINRATE_VS_KCALL}} ({{P2_WINRATE_VS_KCALL_CI}}).

What we claim

  • With 3–4 controls, a Burst decision takes 0.33–0.52× the time of one pass per control on an H100 (five separate measurements below).
  • On the same weights, Burst and K-call make decisions of indistinguishable offline quality (the interval is too wide to prove a 0.02 margin).
  • In real-time games, where a late answer is a wrong answer, the latency saving turns into longer survival ({{P2_WINRATE_VS_KCALL}} of head-to-heads vs K-call on fresh seeds).

What we do not claim

  • That Burst decides better per decision. When time is frozen (lockstep), it does not beat K-call, and on the helicopter game it was worse.
  • Results beyond these three games, the H100, and the serving path we pinned.
  • A new mechanism. Reading several discrete actions from one pass is known (e.g. OpenVLA-OFT, action branching). This is an engineering combination with an evaluation.
  • That Nagi is a better general model than Jev. Nagi trained on similar control tasks; Jev is a general API.

01 / The problem

Latency is error in control.

A helicopter in a collapsing cave has three controls: collective, cyclic and winch. The game advances every 250 ms whether or not the model has answered. An answer that arrives late acts on a state that no longer exists; an answer that has not arrived means the previous controls keep running.

Nagi answers a question by reading option-letter probabilities from one forward pass. With K controls, the plain way is K-call: one forward pass per control, one after the other. At three controls that is three passes per tick, and on an H100 they do not fit in 250 ms.

This is not a small effect. In our previous test the same K-call model scored R = 0.324 on the helicopter game when time was frozen and 0.108 when it had to keep up with the clock. It survived 75.1 s on average frozen (24 seeds) and 22.9 s in real time (5 seeds). So we rank models only in real time: the world never pauses, and latency is never subtracted or normalized away. A benchmark that freezes time measures a product nobody can run.

The frozen real-time rules (same for every player)
  • Clock: 4 Hz (250 ms ticks). At each tick, collect a finished reply, then send a new request on the current state if none is in flight. One request in flight per stream.
  • A reply is applied at the first tick after it arrives. A reply older than 1,000 ms is stale: rejected and logged.
  • Persistent controls hold their last value; after 1,000 ms without an applied reply they reset to the game's safe default (an expiry reset).
  • If the harness itself processes a tick more than 100 ms late, the round is void: replayed once with the same seed, then dropped from both sides of every comparison. More than 1 void in a 10-round screen invalidates the whole cell.
  • Latency is measured end to end on the serving path: prompt rendering, tokenization, forward pass(es), readout and, for an API, the network round trip.

02 / How Burst works

K answer slots, one pass.

The readout

Burst renders one prompt: the state, then every control with its lettered options, then an answer block with one slot per control. Each slot is a marker [k] followed by a blank token □:

STATE:
<state>

CONTROLS (choose all at the same time):
[1] collective: A) <option>  B) <option>  C) <option>
[2] cyclic: A) …  B) …  C) …  D) …
[3] winch: A) …  B) …  C) …

ANSWER:
[1]□
[2]□
[3]□

One forward pass over this prompt. For slot k we take the hidden state hk at the last token of [k] and project it only onto the letter tokens of that control's options, through the model's own output head:

pk = softmax( softcap(hk · Wletters(k)) / T ), answer = argmax. T = 1.0.

The token after each [k] is always the blank, never an answer, so no slot sees another slot's choice. The K distributions are read in parallel and are conditionally independent given the prompt. Training targets for each slot are the max-marginals of an exact joint oracle, so that per-slot argmax recovers the best joint action when it is unique. Ties are the known failure mode of this design, and they matter below.

03 / Evidence

Survive longer when the clock is real.

Arena v3 is a set of three fail-first games: difficulty rises every tick, on the same schedule for every player, until everyone fails (cap 60 s, six 10 s levels). The metric is simple: on the same seed, whoever fails later wins that head-to-head; a tie counts ½. We average over opponents, then over games. It is invariant to how the difficulty ramp is shaped.

Snake Rush
1 control (direction). Speed and walls ramp up. Burst has nothing to save here.
Rotorwash-Ramp
3 controls (collective, cyclic, winch). Helicopter in a collapsing cave.
Booster Gauntlet
4 controls. Consecutive rocket landings with rising wind and heavier engines. The least-seen game for Nagi.

    Same weights: the gain is speed, not judgment

    Because Burst and K-call share weights, comparing them isolates the readout. Burst's first test gives a consistent picture: with time frozen, the two make decisions of the same quality, and K-call even gets three passes per move to Burst's one. Once the clock is real, Burst is ahead because its answers arrive in time.

    Versus Jev: slower, but better decisions

    Jev (version 1.13.0, a commercial API) answers all controls in one request, like Burst, and was the fastest player on the two multi-control games: p50 213 and 216 ms including the network, fresh on 88–90% of ticks against Burst's 66%. Burst still survived longer in 70% (Snake Rush), 90% (Rotorwash-Ramp) and 100% (Booster Gauntlet) of head-to-heads in the Board 1 screen. Speed cannot explain that; the difference is in the decisions. The frozen-time test agrees: Burst − Jev on the two-game composite was +0.067 [+0.003, +0.138] over 24 seeds (descriptive, not a preregistered contrast).

    What this benchmark measures, and what it does not

    Measures

    • How long a model keeps a machine alive under rising difficulty, with real latency, on a pinned serving path.
    • Paired comparisons on identical seeds and identical difficulty schedules.
    • Latency, freshness, stale replies and expiry resets per player, per game.

    Does not measure

    • General intelligence, language ability or anything outside these three games.
    • Hardware-matched speed: Jev's hardware is unknown; self-hosted players share one H100 class.
    • Reading brand-new rules: Nagi trained on similar tasks, which we flag rather than hide.

    04 / Limitations and what failed

    The parts that did not work.

    • Failed a preregistered gateBurst was prototyped as “Chord”. That first prototype failed its preregistered order gate: rotating the order in which the controls are listed changed the joint answer on 26.0% of 500 states [18.4%, 34.3%], against a 10% limit. The verdict was REJECT, and it stays REJECT; it is never passed retroactively. The Board 1 numbers above come from that prototype readout.
    • The fix, tested as a new candidateTwo variants, with gates frozen before any data: A always lists controls in a canonical order, so the deployed answer cannot depend on the caller's order (the model itself stays order-sensitive, which A discloses rather than fixes); B renders the state under all K rotations of the control order, scores the K prompts in one batched forward pass and averages each control's letter logits, so every control sits in every slot once. B costs K× the tokens of A. Selected: {{BURST_VARIANT}}, with an order-flip rate of {{P2_ORDER_FLIP_RATE}} (limit {{PREREG_ORDER_LIMIT}}).
    • No coordination gainWe hoped reading all controls together would improve coordination between them. It did not: the preregistered “clear win” test on coordination states failed (MA2 −0.016 [−0.113, +0.072], MA2b +0.010 [−0.078, +0.088]).
    • Worse flying with time frozenIn frozen-time Rotorwash, Burst hit the cave ceiling in 24 of 24 rounds and survived 43.1 s less than K-call [−53.0, −32.0]. The real-time advantage exists in spite of this.
    • Screen-level evidenceBoard 1 is a screen: 10 seeds per game. Burst was picked as the best of four Nagi entries after the results, which inflates its numbers (winner's curse), and two of the four share weights. That is why the confirmation above runs on fresh seeds under a preregistration.
    • A player we could not rankCLM-8B was entered but failed its own conformance gate (rule D15), so it is not ranked. Board 1 therefore has 6 players, and Burst was selected against 2 external models (Jev, SemIf-4B), not 3.
    • In-domain trainingBurst's weights, K-call's and ENORMOUS-CL's were trained on similar machine tasks (hover, crane, closed-loop snake). This was intended and is flagged on every row. No game engine, seed or state from the Arena appears in training (audited). External players' training is unknown.
    • A timing wall in Booster GauntletEach landing must happen within 22 s, and many of the strongest rounds end at exactly 22.0 s. Those head-to-heads are ties, so Booster separates strong from weak players more than it separates the strong ones.
    • One control, no gainWith K = 1 (Snake Rush) Burst does one pass either way and is slightly slower (227 vs 211 ms p50) because its prompt is longer.
    • The baseline is sequentialK-call here is the SDK default: K forward passes one after another. We did not measure a K-call that batches its K prompts or caches the shared state prefix. Such a baseline would close part of the latency gap, at the cost of more tokens.
    • Hardware and servingAll self-hosted numbers: H100 80 GB, bf16 weights with the adapter merged, fused kernels, 3 streams per GPU. Other GPUs, quantization or batch sizes will give other numbers. Nothing here is a service-level guarantee.
    • Training historyA preregistered pause fired at step 125 of 417 (language benchmark −2.07 points) and was overridden by the project owner; the run continued. At the final step the language score is −0.5 points vs the previous ENORMOUS (0.8578 vs 0.8631, n = 200).
    • ProbabilitiesTemperature 1.0 is not learned calibration; probabilities can be overconfident on new domains. Held-out genres in the offline test rest on 2 families only.
    • Who ran thisNagi's developers designed, ran and analysed every test on this page. Everything needed to check it is linked below.
